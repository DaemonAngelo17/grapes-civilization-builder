## 1. System Architecture & Pedagogical Model

The **G.R.A.P.E.S. Civilization Builder & Defense Simulator** models ancient civilization development through deep environmental, technological, and socio-political causation.

### Core Subsystems:
1. **Expanded Global Taxonomy:** 31 historical questions organized into the 6 G.R.A.P.E.S. domains containing **300 deeply researched options** drawn from ancient archaeological records across Africa, Eurasia, the Americas, and Oceania.
2. **Universal Multi-Choice Architecture:** Every question uses multi-choice checkboxes, removing arbitrary selection limits while strictly bounding valid combinations via historical logic.
3. **Grade 7 Logic Guard:** An active validation layer enforcing **over 260 mutual exclusivity rules**, preventing anachronisms and geographic impossibilities.
4. **Intelligent Auto-Select Synergy Engine:** Automatically links historical prerequisites when foundational choices are made (e.g. selecting Outrigger War Canoes auto-adds Catamaran Engineering).
5. **Synthesis Engine:** Compiles user choices into a 6-paragraph historical narrative, a quick-reference oral defense fact sheet, and 5 customized teacher cross-examination defense questions.

### 2.2 Three Civilization Scales

#### Independent City-State (Local: ~10,000–50,000 citizens) (`city_state`)
- **Territorial Scope:** A single fortified city with surrounding farm fields (like early Ur, Athens, Sparta, or Tikal). Confined to ONE local geographic terrain and one water source. Citizens can assemble directly in person.
- **Capacity Limits & Restrictions:**
  - Max Terrains Allowed: **1**
  - Max Freshwater Systems: **1**
  - Can Build Continental Great Wall: **❌ Prohibited (Requires Continental Empire)**
  - Can Operate Direct Citizen Democracy: **✅ Permitted**
  - Multi-Province Satrapy Bureaucracy: **❌ Prohibited (City-states lack distant foreign provinces)**

#### Regional Kingdom or League (Medium: ~100,000–1,000,000 people) (`regional_kingdom`)
- **Territorial Scope:** A unified river valley, peninsula, or federation of allied cities (like Old Kingdom Egypt, the Delian League, or Shang China). Can span up to 2 neighboring terrain zones.
- **Capacity Limits & Restrictions:**
  - Max Terrains Allowed: **2**
  - Max Freshwater Systems: **2**
  - Can Build Continental Great Wall: **❌ Prohibited (Requires Continental Empire)**
  - Can Operate Direct Citizen Democracy: **❌ Prohibited (Citizens cannot physically assemble over large distances)**
  - Multi-Province Satrapy Bureaucracy: **✅ Permitted**

#### Grand Continental Empire (Mega: Millions of subjects across biomes) (`continental_empire`)
- **Territorial Scope:** A colossal multi-ethnic superstate spanning multiple climates, mountain ranges, and seas (like the Roman Empire, Persian Empire, Han China, or Inca Empire). Requires provincial governors and royal roads.

## 3. Exhaustive Question & Option Taxonomy (300 Verbatim Options)

### Geography (G)
*The physical land, rivers, rocks, trees, and weather that shape how your people survive and adapt.*

#### G1. Dominant Terrain & Topography (Where do your people live?)
- **ID:** `g2_terrain` | **Category:** `G` | **Options Count:** 11
- **Teacher Guidance:** Choose your land types. A City-State is confined to 1 local terrain, while a Grand Empire can span multiple biomes across continents!
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"Think about real history: Greece had rocky peninsulas and mountains (so they became sailors!), while Mesopotamia had flat river valleys (so they built huge mud-brick cities)."*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Flat, featureless alluvial floodplains (great for farming, terrible for defense)
  2. Steep, jagged mountain ranges (requires terrace farming, provides natural fortresses)
  3. Arid desert expanses surrounded by narrow fertile river strips
  4. Dense, humid tropical rainforest with thick canopy
  5. Rocky peninsula with jagged coastlines and hundreds of small islands (like Greece)
  6. High-altitude flat plateau (steppe) swept by strong winds
  7. Fertile volcanic caldera valleys with nutrient-rich soils and crater lakes (like the Valley of Mexico)
  8. Vast river delta marshlands and bayous intersected by tidal estuaries
  9. Elevated temperate highland savanna with fertile volcanic basalt plateaus and acacia woodlands (like the Ethiopian highlands / Kingdom of Aksum)
  10. Coastal fog oasis and hyper-arid desert scrub bordered by rich cold-water ocean upwelling currents (like the Peruvian coast / Caral & Paracas)
  11. Volcanic oceanic high islands and coral ring atolls surrounded by vast open ocean (like Polynesia / Tonga / Hawaii)

#### G2. Primary Freshwater Source (How do your people get drinking & farm water?)
- **ID:** `g1_freshwater` | **Category:** `G` | **Options Count:** 11
- **Teacher Guidance:** Every ancient civilization needed fresh water. Without water, crops wither and everyone dies of thirst. How does your civilization get its water?
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"In Egypt, the Nile flooded gently at the exact same time every year, so farmers knew when to plant. In Mesopotamia, the Tigris and Euphrates flooded violently with no warning!"*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Predictable annual flooding river (like the Nile — leaves rich black silt, easy to plan around)
  2. Violent, unpredictable twin rivers (like the Tigris/Euphrates — requires heavy levee and canal work)
  3. Underground limestone sinkholes and aquifers (like Mayan cenotes — no surface rivers)
  4. High-altitude glacial mountain meltwater (like the Andes — cold, fast-flowing streams)
  5. Seasonal monsoon rains and rainwater harvesting reservoirs (like the Indus/Khmer)
  6. Oasis springs scattered across an arid desert plateau
  7. Braided loess river basin prone to massive silt accumulation and devastating course shifts (like the Yellow River / Huang He)
  8. Karst subterranean river systems and highland limestone springs (like southwest China and the Balkans)
  9. Tropical crater caldera lakes and seasonal interconnected marshland lagoons (like Lake Texcoco and the Valley of Oaxaca)
  10. Desert flash-flood wadis managed through dry-river canyon dams and terraced retention basins (like Nabataea / Petra)
  11. Basalt island freshwater lenses, volcanic crater springs, and coconut/breadfruit water storage (like Pacific atolls and high islands)

#### G3. Natural Defensive Barriers (What protects your borders from invaders?)
- **ID:** `g3_barriers` | **Category:** `G` | **Options Count:** 10
- **Teacher Guidance:** Select the geographic features that protect your land. If nature doesn’t protect you, you must build giant walls and keep a huge army ready at all times.
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"Ancient Egypt was protected by giant deserts on both sides and waterfalls (cataracts) in the south, so they went hundreds of years without being invaded! Mesopotamia had flat open plains, so they were constantly attacked."*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Impassable, freezing mountain ranges blocking the border
  2. Vast, waterless deserts that starve invading armies before they arrive
  3. Treacherous rocky coastlines, reefs, and stormy seas
  4. Dense, disease-ridden jungles (malaria/venomous wildlife)
  5. Cataracts (shallow, boulder-filled river rapids that stop enemy boats)
  6. None — completely open flat plains requiring man-made walls and constant military vigilance
  7. Deep river chasms and impassable canyon gorges crossed only by suspension bridges
  8. Expansive swamps and treacherous mangrove flats that swallow heavy supply wagons and cavalry
  9. Uncharted offshore open ocean expanses requiring advanced celestial wayfinding to cross (island sanctuary isolation)
  10. Towering vertical sandstone cliffs and slot canyon gorges with hidden bottleneck canyon entrances (the Siq of Petra)

#### G4. Abundant Natural Resources (What useful materials does nature give you?)
- **ID:** `g4_resources` | **Category:** `G` | **Options Count:** 13
- **Teacher Guidance:** Select the raw materials abundant in your land. Note: High-grade iron ore was only discovered and smelted during the Iron Age (after 1200 BCE)!
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"The Sumerians had almost no stone or wood, so they made everything—even their school tablets and houses—out of river mud and clay! The Maya had sharp black volcanic glass (obsidian) for swords."*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Rich alluvial clay and mud (for bricks, pottery, and writing tablets)
  2. Papyrus reeds, bamboo, or marsh plants (for paper, boats, and baskets)
  3. Quarried limestone, granite, or marble (for permanent monuments)
  4. Dense cedar, oak, or mahogany forests (for shipbuilding and palace roofs)
  5. Copper and tin deposits (for smelting bronze weapons and tools)
  6. High-grade iron ore (for superior armor and agricultural plows)
  7. Gold, silver, jade, or lapis lazuli (for luxury goods and religious idols)
  8. Obsidian (volcanic glass for razor-sharp blades and surgical tools)
  9. Natural bitumen and surface asphalt seeps (for caulking boats, water-sealing bricks, and embalming)
  10. Vast rock salt domes and coastal evaporation salterns (for meat/fish preservation and vital trade leverage)
  11. Wild silk cocoons and fine white mulberry groves (for imperial silk weaving and thread)
  12. Marine murex sea snail beds (for extracting prized royal Tyrian purple dye)
  13. Basalt, andesite, and diorite volcanic stones (for monumental carving and hard stone grinding tools)

#### G5. Critical Resource Shortages (What essential item are you MISSING?)
- **ID:** `g5_shortages` | **Category:** `G` | **Options Count:** 9
- **Teacher Guidance:** No ancient civilization had everything. What crucial item did your people have to travel far away to trade for or conquer neighbors to get?
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"Egypt had plenty of gold and stone, but had almost no tall trees to build big cargo ships or palace roofs. They had to sail to Lebanon to buy giant cedar logs!"*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Lack of tall timber for shipbuilding and large roofs (like Egypt/Mesopotamia)
  2. Lack of hard stone or metal ores (only have mud and clay)
  3. Lack of arable farmland (too mountainous or rocky to grow enough grain)
  4. Lack of horses or pack animals for transport and cavalry
  5. Lack of salt for preserving meat and fish
  6. Lack of tin or alloy fluxes (crippling bronze tool and weapon manufacture unless sustained by maritime trade)
  7. Lack of fuel firewood and charcoal (forcing reliance on dried animal dung or severely limiting metal smelting)
  8. Lack of textile plant fibers or sheep wool (forcing reliance on animal skins, bark cloth, or expensive imports)
  9. Lack of domestic clay deposits for pottery (requiring carving wooden vessels, stone gourds, or importing ceramic ware)

#### G6. Recurring Environmental Disasters (What natural danger threatens your people?)
- **ID:** `g6_disasters` | **Category:** `G` | **Options Count:** 10
- **Teacher Guidance:** Select the environmental disasters that happen regularly. These disasters tested whether the king was doing his job and if the gods were angry!
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"In China, when giant floods broke the Yellow River dikes, people believed the gods had revoked the emperor’s "Mandate of Heaven" because he failed to protect the kingdom."*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Catastrophic, unannounced river floods that wash away entire villages
  2. Multi-year severe droughts that dry up canals and cause famine
  3. Frequent tectonic earthquakes and volcanic eruptions
  4. Locust swarms and agricultural blights
  5. Monsoons, typhoons, or coastal tsunamis
  6. Soil salinization (over-irrigation making the soil too salty to grow crops)
  7. Encroaching desert sand dunes and hyper-arid dust storms suffocating frontier farmland
  8. Rapid silting of canal choke points and violent shifting of navigable river channels
  9. Sudden El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) ocean temperature spikes triggering torrential desert deluge rains and collapsing marine fisheries
  10. Severe winter blizzards and permafrost frosts (dzud) freezing pasture grasslands and devastating herds of livestock

### Economy (E)
*How your civilization feeds its people, produces goods, trades with foreign neighbors, and collects taxes.*

#### E1. Staple Agricultural Crops (What food feeds your growing cities?)
- **ID:** `e1_crops` | **Category:** `E` | **Options Count:** 10
- **Teacher Guidance:** Pick your main crops. In ancient history, an empire is only as strong as its farmers. If crops fail, cities starve and empires collapse.
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"Mesopotamia and Egypt grew wheat and barley for bread and beer. Ancient China relied on rice and millet. In the Americas, indigenous farmers grew the "Three Sisters" (corn, beans, and squash together!)."*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Wheat and Barley (for bread, porridge, and brewing beer — standard in fertile crescents)
  2. Wet-paddy Rice and Millet (high-calorie crops supporting massive populations)
  3. Maize (Corn), Beans, and Squash ("Three Sisters" grown together symbiotically)
  4. Freeze-dried Potatoes (Chuño) and Quinoa (high-altitude mountain crops)
  5. Olives and Grapes (grown in rocky soil to press into high-value olive oil and wine for export)
  6. Date Palms and Figs (drought-resistant sweet staples producing timber, fibers, and wine in desert oases)
  7. Sorghum, Yams, and Teff (heat-tolerant grains cultivated along tropical and savannah river courses)
  8. Cotton and Flax / Linseed (fibers for high-volume textile industries, sails, and oil presses)
  9. Taro, Yams, and Breadfruit (starchy root and tree staples cultivated on volcanic terraces and wetland ponds)
  10. Pastoral Transhumance & Herd Grazing (minimal grain farming; diet of roasted meat, fermented mare's milk [kumis], cheese, and wild steppe grains)

#### E2. Domesticated Animals (Which animals help your people work, eat, or travel?)
- **ID:** `e2_animals` | **Category:** `E` | **Options Count:** 11
- **Teacher Guidance:** Domesticated animals gave ancient people meat, milk, wool, leather, and muscles to pull heavy wagons and plows.
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"In the Americas, the Maya and Aztecs had NO horses, oxen, or cows! Everything had to be carried on the backs of human porters or by canoe. In the Andes, the Inca used llamas."*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Oxen and Water Buffalo (heavy draft animals for pulling plows and wagons)
  2. Sheep and Goats (for wool, milk, cheese, and leather in rocky terrain)
  3. Horses and Camels (for aristocratic cavalry, messengers, and desert caravan trade)
  4. Llamas and Alpacas (light pack animals and source of warm mountain wool; no heavy wheeled carts)
  5. None — all goods are carried on the backs of human porters and canoes (like the Maya/Aztec)
  6. Donkeys and Mules (sure-footed pack equines carrying bulky freight through mountain passes)
  7. War Hounds and Herding Dogs (bred for guarding storehouses, palace security, and battlefield support)
  8. Domesticated Dromedary & Bactrian Camels (enduring desert caravanners able to carry 400 lbs across waterless sand for a week)
  9. Asian War Elephants & Work Elephants (trained for heavy logging, clearing jungle tracks, and terrifying battlefield shock)
  10. Vast Herds of Sanga & Zebu Cattle (walking banks of wealth, milk, blood, leather, and royal bride-price prestige)
  11. Domesticated Pacific Pigs, Red Jungle Fowl (Chickens), and Poi Dogs (transported across oceans in voyaging canoes)

#### E3. Trade Networks & Exports (How do your merchants trade with foreign nations?)
- **ID:** `e5_trade` | **Category:** `E` | **Options Count:** 8
- **Teacher Guidance:** No ancient society could survive alone. How did your merchants move goods to trade for resources your land was missing?
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"The Phoenicians built famous merchant ships that sailed all across the Mediterranean Sea. The Chinese traveled the famous overland Silk Road across deserts and mountains."*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Maritime Trade Empire (merchant fleets sailing across seas to establish trading colonies)
  2. Overland Silk/Incense Caravan Routes (long-distance relay trade across deserts and mountains)
  3. Riverine Network (barges floating downriver with stone/grain and sailing back upriver using wind)
  4. Self-Sufficient Command Economy (foreign trade is banned or strictly controlled by royal agents; everything needed is produced internally)
  5. Vertical Archipelago Exchange (highland and lowland ecological tiers swapping mountain tubers/wool for coastal fish and valley cotton)
  6. Monsoon Indian Ocean Coastal & Open-Sea Dhow Trade (navigating biannual monsoon winds between Africa, Arabia, and India)
  7. Trans-Saharan Gold-Salt Trade (caravans crossing vast sand seas connecting savannah goldfields with coastal salt domes)
  8. Continental River-Confluence Canoe Networks (trading copper plates, mica, lightning-whelk shells, and furs across thousands of miles of rivers)

#### E4. Medium of Exchange / Currency (How do people pay for goods?)
- **ID:** `e3_currency` | **Category:** `E` | **Options Count:** 10
- **Teacher Guidance:** Stamped coins were not invented until around 600 BCE in Lydia! In the Bronze Age, ancient people used barter, weighed silver bullion, or state grain rations.
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"In ancient Egypt, workers building the pyramids were paid in daily rations of bread, beer, and onions from the pharaoh’s royal granary! The Maya used cacao beans (chocolate!) like dollar bills."*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Standardized Stamped Coins (electrum, gold, silver, or bronze coins guaranteed by the state)
  2. Weighed Metal Bullion (chopping silver rings or copper ingots and weighing them on scales for every purchase)
  3. Commodity Money (cacao beans, cowrie shells, salt blocks, or bolts of silk used as cash)
  4. State Grain Redistribution (no money; all farmers give crops to the temple/palace, which hands out daily rations of bread and beer)
  5. Pure Barter & Credit Tallies (trading goods directly or keeping tabs on clay tablets)
  6. Cast Bronze Tool Money (currency cast in the shape of miniature spade blades, knives, or rings)
  7. Bolts of Woven Silk and Standardized Spun Yarn Skeins (used alongside copper cash for official payments and international tribute)
  8. Steatite Inscribed Merchant Seals and Clay Envelope Bullae (merchant authentication counters certifying token transactions)
  9. Wampum Shell Belts, Hammered Copper Plates, and Ceremonial Gift Redistribution (Potlatch / Calumet diplomacy)
  10. Cattle & Livestock Wealth Standard (fines, taxes, royal tribute, and marriage alliances paid in live heads of cattle)

#### E5. Primary Method of Taxation (How does the government get funds & labor?)
- **ID:** `e4_taxation` | **Category:** `E` | **Options Count:** 8
- **Teacher Guidance:** Kings and emperors needed wealth to pay for palaces, walls, and soldier food. How did the government extract wealth from regular people?
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"Ancient governments rarely collected cash. Under the "Corvée" labor system, regular farmers were forced to work for 2 months every year digging canals or hauling pyramid stones!"*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Agricultural Tithe (tax collectors measure field sizes after floods and take 10% to 30% of the harvest)
  2. Corvée Labor System (every peasant family owes 2 months of physical labor per year digging canals or building pyramids during the off-season)
  3. Customs Duties & Trade Tariffs (taxing merchant ships at harbors and caravans at city gates)
  4. State Monopolies (the government owns all salt, iron, and mines; citizens must buy from the state)
  5. Tribute from Conquered Provinces (citizens pay zero taxes because conquered neighbors fund the empire)
  6. Head Tax / Poll Tax (mandatory annual flat tax levied in silver or cloth upon every adult citizen)
  7. Reciprocal Mit'a Labor Service (rotational community work quotas on royal terraces and storehouses in exchange for state festival feasts)
  8. Transit Tolls and Mountain Pass / Bridge Fees (extracting silver and goods from foreign caravans traversing strategic choke points)

### Politics (P)
*Who holds supreme power, how kings or councils govern, how laws are enforced, and how the military defends borders.*

#### P1. System of Government (Who holds supreme political power?)
- **ID:** `p1_gov_system` | **Category:** `P` | **Options Count:** 11
- **Teacher Guidance:** Who makes the ultimate decisions in your society? Note: Direct democracy only works on small city-state scales, while continental empires require provincial administration.
- **Grade 7 History Tip:** *"In Athens, free male citizens voted on every single law in a direct democracy. In Sparta, a military council and two kings ruled. In Egypt, the Pharaoh held total absolute power."*
- **Verbatim Options:**
  1. Centralized Absolute Monarchy (one King/Emperor holds total power over a unified empire)
  2. Decentralized City-States (dozens of independent cities with the same culture that constantly war with each other)
  3. Aristocratic Oligarchy / Republic (ruled by a Senate or Council of wealthy landowning families)
  4. Direct Democracy (all free adult male citizens vote directly on laws and wars in a public assembly)

### 4.2 Civilization Scale Constraints
1. **City-State (`city_state`):**
   - Max **1 terrain** zone (confined to a single valley, delta, or bay).
   - Max **1 primary freshwater** basin.
   - Max **4 abundant resources**.
   - Max **2 staple crops**.
   - Max **2 domesticated animal groups**.
   - Max **2 monuments** (Prohibited from building thousands of miles of Great Walls).
   - Max **3 specialized military doctrines**.
   - **Prohibited:** Satrapy Provincial Administration & Feudal Bureaucracy (no distant foreign provinces).
   - **Prohibited:** Mass Deportations & Imperial Tribute extraction (lacks imperial military logistics).
2. **Regional Kingdom (`regional_kingdom`):**
   - Max **2 neighboring terrain** biomes.
   - Max **2 freshwater** basins.
   - Max **5 abundant resources**.
   - Max **4 staple crops**.
   - Max **4 livestock groups**.
   - Max **4 monuments** (Prohibited from continental Great Walls).
   - **Prohibited:** Direct Citizen Democracy (citizens cannot assemble in person across an entire kingdom).
3. **Continental Empire (`continental_empire`):**
   - Multi-biome permitted.
   - **Prohibited:** Direct Citizen Democracy (citizens cannot walk 2,000 miles to a single town square vote).

### 4.3 Key Environmental & Technological Contradictions
- **Outrigger War Canoes & Catamarans vs Landlocked Mountains:** Outrigger voyaging canoes require open ocean or coastal waterways; locked in purely landlocked mountain plateaus.
- **Outrigger War Canoes vs Timber Shortage:** Large double-hulled catamarans require tall tree trunks for hulls and masts; locked if suffering a tall timber shortage.
- **Nomadic Steppe Confederacy vs Horse Shortages:** Steppe horse confederacies and kurgan burials require horse pastoralism; locked if suffering a horse shortage or relying only on human porters.
- **Cattle Wealth Standard vs Human Porters:** Measuring wealth in live cattle herds contradicts a society that uses zero domesticated animals.
- **Granite Enclosures (Dzimbahwe) & Moai Statues vs Stone Shortage:** Fitting mortarless granite enclosures or carving 14-ton Moai statues requires quarrying hard stone.
- **Chinampas ("Floating Gardens"):** Requires shallow volcanic caldera lakes; locked in waterless deserts and violent river flash-floods.
- **Qanats (Underground Aqueducts):** Built to prevent water evaporation in scorching deserts; useless in soggy tropical rainforests.
- **Quipu Knotted Recording:** Indigenous Andean record-keeping without written words; locks carved stone law pillars, clay cuneiform tablets, and papyrus scrolls (and vice versa).
